## Initial findings (written before reading the author report)

### Theorem A (rational functions)
Steps verified.
1. Regularity. The hypothesis f(kP)=k means f is regular at kP with that value. Then f(Q+P) is regular at kP when f is regular at (k+1)P, so h=f∘τ_P - f - 1 is regular at kP for 1<=k<=N-1 and h(kP)=0. Poles of f may exist elsewhere, including at O; this does not hurt. N<=r-1 ensures kP != O.
2. Degree. τ_P is an automorphism, so deg(f∘τ_P)_∞ = deg(f)_∞ = d, and deg(h)_∞ <= 2d (poles of h lie in poles of f ∪ τ_P^{-1}(poles of f)). The points kP, 1<=k<=N-1<r, are pairwise distinct. A nonzero function has at most deg(h)_0 = deg(h)_∞ distinct zeros, so N-1 <= 2d. Correct.
3. Step "h ≡ 0 gives r=0 in F_p" is INCOMPLETE and its hypothesis "r != p" is the wrong exclusion. Iterating gives f(Q+rP)=f(Q)+r, hence r=0 in the field, i.e. p | r. The excluded case r=p is only one way this happens: p | r with r != p also occurs (p=5, r=10, #E=10, curve y^2=x^3+3x, P=(2,2); found by exhaustive search, see EA/E0 output). For p>=7, p|r and r != p is impossible by Hasse (r>=2p > p+1+2√p). Hence the written proof has a gap for p=5, r=10.
   Repair that removes the hypothesis altogether: if h ≡ 0 then f(Q+P)=f(Q)+1, f is nonconstant, so it has a pole; the pole set S satisfies τ_P^{-1}S=S and is a union of <P>-orbits of size r, so d >= r > (N-1)/2. So Theorem A holds for every r (including r=p anomalous), with no hypothesis r != p. The only genuine requirements: p > 3 (short Weierstrass) and N<=r-1.
4. Polynomial case. f in F[X,Y] of total degree d has its only pole at O, with order <= 3d (attained by Y^d). Hence 2·3d >= N-1, i.e. d >= (N-1)/6. Correct, and the constant cannot be improved by this argument: ord_O(X^aY^b)=2a+3b. Reducing Y^2 via the curve equation raises total degree, so it gives no better pole bound per unit degree. (Heuristic count: polynomials of degree d mod the curve form a space of dimension about 3d, so interpolation at N points is expected at d about N/3; the bound N/6 is within a factor 2.)
5. Tightness of the general bound: u/v with u,v in L(mO), 2m >= N+1 gives a solution of the linear system; numerics find nondegenerate interpolants with pole degree m = (N-1)/2 exactly in some cases (e.g. p=11, N=5, m=2). So the constant 1/2 is essentially best possible for rational functions (not for all N).

### Theorem A' (SLP)
- Each operation g1 op g2 (+,-,×,÷) satisfies deg(g)_∞ <= deg(g1)_∞ + deg(g2)_∞ (for ÷: poles of g1 plus zeros of g2, and deg of zero divisor = deg of pole divisor). Inputs x,y have degrees 2 and 3; constants 0. So after L operations degree <= 3·2^L. Combined with A: 3·2^L >= (N-1)/2, i.e. L >= log2((N-1)/6). The stated bound log2((N-1)/12) is therefore TRUE but weaker by exactly 1 (valid, not sharp; the extra factor 2 is unexplained).
- "Computes for all Q=kP" must be read as: every intermediate division has nonzero denominator at every kP. Then every intermediate function is regular at kP (induction), and the final value equals the function f ∈ F_q(E) evaluated at kP. An intermediate that is formally nonzero in F_q(x,y) but identically zero on E (multiple of y^2-x^3-ax-b) makes the SLP undefined at all points, so is excluded. Conventions like x/0=0 are outside the model and invalidate the argument. The SLP must be read as a program on E (not on the polynomial ring), constants arbitrary in F_q (non-uniform, may depend on E,P); q = p^m is fine since f ∈ F̄_p(E).
- Additionally requires N<=r-1 and N>=2 as in A; log2((N-1)/12) is negative (vacuous) for N<=13.
- "Ω(n^2) bit cost": a valid deduction only under the explicit hypothesis that every field operation costs >= c·log q bit operations (n = log p ≈ log r because r <= (√p+1)^2) and N = r-1. Then L >= n - O(1) gives cost >= c·n·(n-O(1)). This hypothesis is a model assumption, not a theorem; it is plausible (an operation with a non-constant operand must read n bits) but it says nothing about algorithms outside straight-line algebraic programs (no comparisons, table lookups, bit extraction, randomization, BSGS, rho). The resulting bound is weaker than the known generic-group Ω(√r) bound in a different model and is not comparable to it; it should not be presented as a bit-complexity lower bound for ECDLP.

### Theorem B
Reconstruction. Let F=g(x(Q)), even, pole order 2m (m=deg g) at O. Put H(Q)=F(Q+P)+F(Q-P)-2F(Q)-2.
- H(kP)=(k+1)^2+(k-1)^2-2k^2-2=0 for 2<=k<=(r-3)/2 (needs (k+1)<=(r-1)/2; k=1 fails because F(O) is undefined). That is (r-5)/2 values.
- H is even, so also H(-kP)=0. For r odd the points ±kP, 2<=k<=(r-3)/2, are pairwise distinct (k+j<r, 2k<r), giving r-5 distinct zeros.
- Poles of H: orders 2m at each of P, -P, O (distinct points since r>=3), with coefficients 1,1,-2 (nonzero for p odd). So deg(H)_∞ = 6m exactly (if m>=1), H != 0 automatically, and m=0 is impossible when r>=5, p>3 (k=1,2 give values 1 and 4). Hence r-5 <= 6m, m >= (r-5)/6.
Conclusion: the stated claim is correct. Corrections: (i) "r prime" is not needed; r odd and r>=5 suffices (distinctness of x(kP), k<=(r-1)/2, only needs r odd); (ii) p odd (automatic) and p>3 so that 1 != 4; (iii) the range (r-1)/2 is exactly the natural one: for k beyond it F(kP)=(r-k)^2 (evenness), not k^2, so the hypothesis is the correct one; (iv) no condition relating p and r (the identity is over Z). Side remark: at k=(r-1)/2 the second difference equals -r mod p, so when p | r (r=p anomalous) one extra zero gives (r-3)/2 zeros hence a marginally better bound, immaterial. Gap to truth: Lagrange interpolation gives deg <= (r-3)/2 and generically equals it, so the claim is off by a factor of 3 from the generic truth (not claimed sharp).
